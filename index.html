Section 01 :: Theory & Algorithm

LEFT RECURSION

Understanding Left-Recursive Context-Free Grammars, top-down parsing constraints, and mathematical elimination algorithms.

Left Recursion is a property of a Context-Free Grammar (CFG) in which a non-terminal appears as the leftmost symbol on the right-hand side of its own production. Such a grammar is called a left-recursive grammar.
📜

What is Context-Free Grammar (CFG)?

A formal grammar G = (V, T, P, S) that specifies the syntax of programming languages, comprising Non-terminals (V), Terminals (T), Production Rules (P), and a Start symbol (S).

🏷️

What is a Non-terminal?

Variables or syntactic placeholders (such as E, T, F, A) that can be iteratively expanded into strings of terminal symbols and other non-terminals.

⬅️

Why Non-terminal Appears as Leftmost?

In a production rule like A → Aα, the non-terminal A sits as the very first symbol on the right-hand side, meaning expanding A immediately begins with A itself.

🔄

Why this Grammar is Called Left Recursive?

Because the recursion occurs strictly on the leftmost position. Derivations continually expand to the left indefinitely without consuming any terminal tokens from the input.

⚠

Why Left Recursion Creates Problems for Top-Down Parsing?

Top-down parsers (like recursive-descent) implement a function for each non-terminal. In A → Aα, procedure A() calls A() endlessly without reading an input token, causing an infinite loop and stack overflow.

✨

Why Elimination of Left Recursion is Necessary?

To transform left-recursive rules into equivalent right-recursive rules, allowing predictive top-down LL(1) parsers to parse source code deterministically without looping forever.

Types of Left Recursion

Direct Left Recursion

Immediate Call

Occurs when a non-terminal calls itself immediately in a single production rule.

General Form:
A → Aα | β
A The non-terminal symbol undergoing direct left recursion.
α Any non-empty string of terminals and non-terminals (α ≠ ε) trailing A.
β Any string of terminals and non-terminals that does NOT begin with A.

Indirect Left Recursion

Mutual Chain

Occurs through a cyclic sequence of two or more non-terminals (A expands to B, which eventually expands back to A).

General Form:
A → Bα
B → Aβ
Derivation Trace:
A ⇒ Bα ⇒ Aβα
Here, A derives a sentential form starting with A through intermediate non-terminal B. Indirect left recursion is eliminated by ordering non-terminals and substituting earlier productions.

Algorithm to Eliminate Left Recursion

1
Identify recursive productions
Scan all productions of non-terminal A to spot alternatives where A appears as the leftmost symbol.
2
Separate recursive and non-recursive productions
Group the productions into recursive alternatives (A → Aα₁, Aα₂, ...) and non-recursive base alternatives (A → β₁, β₂, ...).
3
Introduce new non-terminal A'
Create a new auxiliary non-terminal symbol A' to carry the right-recursive repetition.
4
Rewrite grammar
Write: A → β₁A' | β₂A' | ... and
A' → α₁A' | α₂A' | ... | ε
5
Final grammar becomes non-left recursive
The newly synthesized grammar generates the exact same language while being completely right-recursive and top-down friendly.
STANDARD ARITHMETIC GRAMMAR EXAMPLE CLASSIC COMPILER TEST CASE

Original Grammar

E → E + T | T
T → T * F | F
F → ( E ) | id

Step-by-Step Elimination

Step A: For non-terminal E → E + T | T, we have α = + T and β = T.
Applying algorithm yields: E → T E' and E' → + T E' | ε.

Step B: For non-terminal T → T * F | F, we have α = * F and β = F.
Applying algorithm yields: T → F T' and T' → * F T' | ε.

Step C: For non-terminal F → ( E ) | id, there is no left recursion. The productions remain unchanged.

Before Elimination
E → E + T | T
T → T * F | F
F → ( E ) | id
✖ Causes infinite recursive call loop in top-down parser.
➔
Final Non-Left-Recursive Grammar
E → T E'
E' → + T E' | ε
T → F T'
T' → * F T' | ε
F → ( E ) | id
✓ Perfectly suited for recursive-descent and LL(1) parsers.
Section 02 :: Theory & Algorithm

LEFT FACTORING

Resolving common left prefixes to eliminate parser ambiguity and backtracking in predictive LL(1) compilers.

If the productions are in the form
A → αβ₁ | αβ₂ | αβ₃ | γ₁ | γ₂
then we say that the production is left factored or the process is called left factoring.
The grammar having common left prefixes is called an ambiguous grammar, and left factoring is used to remove this ambiguity.
🔤

What is Common Prefix?

A common prefix (α) is an identical initial sequence of terminals or non-terminals shared by two or more alternative production bodies of the same non-terminal symbol.

❓

What is Ambiguous Grammar?

A grammar where a predictive parser cannot make a deterministic choice with 1-token lookahead because multiple production choices begin identically with the same prefix.

🔀

Why Ambiguity Occurs?

When seeing token α on the input tape, the parser cannot decide whether to expand to αβ₁ or αβ₂ without guessing or performing costly backtracking.

🛡️

Why Left Factoring Removes Ambiguity?

By factoring out the common prefix α, the decision is postponed: the parser first consumes α, and only then chooses between β₁ and β₂ based on the next token.

🎯

Importance in LL(1) Parser

An LL(1) parser requires disjoint FIRST sets for all alternatives of a non-terminal. Left factoring ensures distinct lookaheads, creating valid LL(1) parsing tables without collisions.

Algorithm for Left Factoring

1
Find common prefix
For non-terminal A, identify the longest common prefix string α among two or more of its alternative bodies.
2
Remove common prefix
Strip the common prefix α from each matching alternative to isolate remaining suffixes (β₁, β₂, ...).
3
Introduce new non-terminal
Create a new helper non-terminal A' to derive all remaining suffix options.
4
Rewrite productions
Define A → α A' | γ (where γ represents alternatives without prefix α), and
A' → β₁ | β₂ | ...
5
Final left factored grammar
Repeat the process until no two productions for any non-terminal share a common prefix.
STANDARD CONDITIONAL STATEMENT GRAMMAR (DANGLING-ELSE) EXACT ACADEMIC SPECIFICATION

Example

Consider the given grammar G:

G = (V,T,P,S)
Where
V = {S,E}
T = {a,b,e,i,t}

Productions
S → iEtS | iEtSeS | a
E → b

Solution

The common prefix is iEtS.
So after left factoring:

S → iEtSS' | a
S' → eS | ε
E → b

Final Left Factored Grammar

S → iEtSS' | a
S' → eS | ε
E → b
